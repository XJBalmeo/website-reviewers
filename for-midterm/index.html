Introduction to Research

What is Research?

The term combines "re" (again/anew) and "search" (to examine closely). It is a structured, systematic inquiry that uses scientific methods to solve problems.

Put simply, research is finding answers to questions in an organized way so that others can trust your results. You aren't guessing; you are following a strict process (the scientific method) to show that something is true.

Characteristics of Good Research

  • Empirical: Based on hard, observable evidence, not just opinions.
  • Systematic: Follows a step-by-step, logical order.
  • Controlled: You block out outside distractions to test one specific thing.
  • Valid & Verifiable: Your results are correct, and if someone else repeats your exact experiment, they get the same results.

Analogy: Investigating a Car Crash (Positivist vs. Phenomenalist)

There are two main ways to approach research. Imagine you are investigating a car crash:

Positivist (Quantitative)

You measure the skid marks, calculate the speed from physics, and record the exact time of day. You only care about hard numbers and facts, and you believe there is one absolute truth (the driver was going 65 mph).

Phenomenalist (Qualitative)

You interview the drivers to ask how they felt, what distracted them, and what they experienced. You care about the human experience and story, and you believe truth is subjective and depends on personal perspective.

The Thesis

An academic document that makes a claim based on theories. It requires causality and the empirical testing of a hypothesis. For example, proving that a brand-new sorting algorithm reduces processing time by 20% compared to an older algorithm.

The Feasibility Study

An exercise to test practical implementation. It assesses real-world parameters like financial returns, supply, and demand. For example, determining if it is financially profitable to build a new paid parking lot next to the university campus.

Conducting a Research

Summary vs. Synthesis

When writing a literature review, the biggest mistake students make is just summarizing. You must synthesize.

A Summary

Recaps the important information from a single source. It tells the reader exactly what the original author said.

Example: "Author A says computers are fast. Author B says computers are getting cheaper."

A Synthesis

Re-organizes information from multiple sources to show trends, debates, or gaps that inform your specific problem.

Example: "While Author A focuses on the speed of computers, Author B highlights their decreasing cost. Together, this indicates a trend towards accessible, high-speed computing..."

1. Primary Sources

Accounts of the actual research that was done, such as original journal articles, a researcher's raw data, autobiographies, or personal diaries.

2. Secondary Sources

"Once removed" from the actual research. These are scholarly summaries of primary works, such as textbooks, review papers, and encyclopedias.

3. Tertiary Sources

Sources that give clues about where to find references, such as bibliographies, guidebooks, indexes, or library catalogs.

Technique: Chain Searching

Also known as backward searching. You find one good, recent research paper. Instead of searching Google Scholar for more, you look at that paper's "References" list at the end and follow those older references to find the foundational studies. You are following the "chain" backward in time.

Research Methodology

The WRONG Way to Formulate a Problem (Common in IT/CS)

The Mistake: Deciding you want to build a specific app (e.g., "A student attendance tracker app"). You build the tool, and then try to find literature to justify why attendance is a problem.

The Correct Way: Review literature to identify a true gap or problem (e.g., "Teachers are losing 20 minutes a day to manual attendance"). Review existing solutions to make sure you aren't repeating work. Then propose a novel software tool as the implementation of your solution.

The SMART Criteria

A good research problem must follow these rules:

S

Specific

The question states exactly which variables are to be determined. Avoid vague language. Not "I want to study students," but "I want to study the math test scores of 9th-grade public school students."

M

Measurable

The variables can be measured with appropriate instruments or tools. You can't easily measure "how much a student loves school," but you can measure "the number of days a student is absent."

A

Attainable

The objective can realistically be reached because you can actually collect the needed data. A study that requires interviewing the President of the Philippines is probably not attainable for undergraduates.

R

Relevant

Results can be obtained through scientific procedures and the study adds value to the field. It must solve a real problem that people care about, not a trivial one.

T

Time-bound

There is a realistic timeframe for every step of the research. A study that tracks a person's behavior for 10 years is not suitable for a one-semester class.

Research Classifications (By Objectives)

1. Descriptive

Systematically describes a situation, problem, or phenomenon. It answers What, When, Where, Who. Example: a survey detailing the dating practices of adolescent girls.

2. Correlational

Attempts to discover or establish a relationship or association between two variables. Example: is there a relationship between hours spent watching TV and a student's GPA?

3. Explanatory (Causal)

Attempts to clarify why and how a relationship exists by looking for direct cause and effect. Example: the exact effect a specific language program has on inner-city children's test scores.

4. Exploratory

Undertaken to explore an area where little is known, usually to test if a larger study is possible. Example: a pilot study to see if a completely new market segment is interested in a product.

Analogy: Theoretical vs. Conceptual Framework

Theoretical Framework: Dwells on time-tested, broad theories (e.g., "Stimulus elicits a response"). Think of it as the general laws of physics and gravity that every architect must follow.

Conceptual Framework: The specific operationalization for your study (e.g., "This specific math software improves test scores for 9th graders"). Think of it as the exact blueprint for the house you are building right now.

Variables & Hypotheses

Variables Defined

A variable is a measurable characteristic that can take on more than one value.

  • Independent (IV): The condition manipulated by the researcher (the cause).
  • Dependent (DV): The outcome measure that is sensitive to changes in the IV (the effect).
  • Control: A variable actively held constant so it doesn't ruin the experiment.
  • Extraneous: An unpredicted outside variable that could impact the DV.
  • Moderator: A variable that alters or masks the true relationship between the IV and DV.
  • Confounding: An "extra" variable not accounted for that secretly causes changes in the DV.

Variable Analogy: Growing a Plant

You want to test if different brands of fertilizer make plants grow taller.

Independent Variable (what you change): The brand of fertilizer used.

Dependent Variable (what you measure): The height the plant grows, in inches.

Control Variables (what you keep the same): The amount of sunlight, the amount of water, and the exact type of dirt. If you don't control the sunlight, you won't know if the fertilizer or the sun caused the growth.

Hypotheses

An educated guess formulated to be tested.

Null Hypothesis (H₀)

A statement of equality or "no difference." It serves as the baseline. Example: "There is no difference in test scores between Group A and B."

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)

A statement of inequality.

Non-directional: "The scores are different."
Directional: "Group A will score higher than Group B."

Concept of Significance

Statistical significance is the degree of risk a researcher is willing to take that a null hypothesis will be rejected by chance.

  • Significance Level (Alpha α): The probability of rejecting H₀ when it is actually true (usually 0.05 or 5%). Chosen before the study.
  • P-value: The probability of obtaining your results if H₀ were true.
  • The Rule: If your P-value is ≤ Alpha (e.g., P = 0.03 and α = 0.05), you reject the Null Hypothesis. Your results are significant.

Statistical Errors: Type I vs. Type II

Because statistics rely on probability, tests can result in errors.

Type I Error (False Positive)

You reject the null hypothesis, but it was actually true. You claim there is an effect, but there isn't.

Medical Example: A doctor tells a biological male, "You are pregnant."

Type II Error (False Negative)

You accept the null hypothesis, but it was actually false. You claim there is no effect, but there really is.

Medical Example: A doctor tells a visibly 9-month pregnant woman, "You are not pregnant."

Analogy: The Boy Who Cried Wolf

H₀ (Baseline): There is no wolf present.

Type I Error (False Positive): The boy cries "Wolf!" when there is no wolf. He raised a false alarm.

Type II Error (False Negative): The boy does nothing when there actually is a wolf. He missed the real threat.
