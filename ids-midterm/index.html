Lesson 1: Introduction to Data Science & Big Data

01
Big Data Foundations

What is Big Data & The Four (4) V’s

What is Big Data?
Extremely large and complex amounts of data that are difficult to collect, store, manage, and analyze using traditional database systems such as RDBMS (MySQL, Oracle, or SQL Server).
In simple terms, Big Data is data that is too large, too fast, or too complex for traditional database tools to handle efficiently.
The Four (4) V’s of Big Data
1
Volume
Means “how much data is there?” Refers to the sheer size and scale of data generated daily (millions or billions of records).
2
Velocity
Means “at what speed is new data generated?” Refers to the rate at which new data flows into an organization in real time (such as streaming video or live sensors).
3
Variety
Means “how diverse are different types of data?” Data comes in structured numeric tables as well as unstructured text documents, video, audio, email, and stock ticker data.
4
Veracity
Means “how accurate is the data?” Refers to the trustworthiness, quality, and reliability of the data.
02
Data Forms

Forms of Big Data: Structured vs. Unstructured Data

Structured Data

1
Definition
Easily analyzed and organized in a database; typically stored in a table where every instance has the same structure (set of attributes).
2
Ease of Use
Easy to find, add, update, or delete because records are highly organized and searchable.
3
Scalability & Analytics
Systems scale easily by adding storage, and data is readily queried with SQL for statistical and machine learning models.
VS

Unstructured Data

1
Definition
Data that does not follow a fixed or organized row-and-column format in a traditional database (makes up 80–90% of all data generated today).
2
Common Examples
Text documents, video, audio, email, stock ticker data, social media posts, and PDFs.
3
Challenges & Analysis
Difficult for traditional RDBMS to store directly; requires Natural Language Processing (NLP) or computer vision to extract meaning.

Data Mining, Data Science & Pattern Discovery

1
Data Mining
The process of examining large amounts of raw data to discover useful information, patterns, relationships, or trends.
If Big Data is a mountain of information, Data Mining is the process of digging through it to find valuable insights.
2
Data Science & Its Ultimate Goal
The study of data to discover useful information, patterns, and insights whose ultimate goal is improving decision making.
Five Fields Combined in Data Science
1

2
Mathematics

3
Data Analysis
Involves examining data to find meaningful information.

Machine Learning

Actionable Insights & Grouping Techniques
1
Actionable Insight (DATA → ANALYSIS → INSIGHT → DECISION)
A finding from data that gives us enough useful information to decide what action should be taken.
2
Customer Segmentation

3
Clustering
The common Data Science and Machine Learning term for grouping entities that share similar characteristics or behaviors.
4
Association-Rule Mining
Data science technique used to discover relationships between items that frequently occur together (such as market basket analysis).

Datasets & Architecture

Core Data Elements, Analytics Record & Data Architecture

1
Data
A collection of facts, observations, or information about something in the real world.
2
Entity (Rows)
The real-world person, object, or event that we are interested in studying (Who or what are we studying?).

Lesson 2: The Data Science Life Cycle

01
Overview & Phase 1

Life Cycle Goals & Phase 1: Business Requirements

What is the Data Science Life Cycle?
A structured process and iterative roadmap of research and discovery that guides a data science project from start to finish in developing predictive models.
Goals: Provide a clear process, improve team-customer communication, use standardized project documents (artifacts), and ensure consistency.
Phase 1: Business Requirements (First & Most Important Phase)
1
Core Focus of Business Requirements
Understand the business problem, define the central goal, and ask the right questions before collecting or analyzing data.
2
Two Important Activities (Business Requirements)
Identify key variables (target variables to be predicted) and identify data sources (where required data originates).
3
Deliverable 1 — Charter Document
A living document that gets updated throughout the project as new data or requirements arise.
4
Deliverable 2 — Data Sources Document
A document which specifies the original and destination locations of the raw data.
5
Deliverable 3 — Data Dictionaries
Provide descriptions of the data supplied by the client, including information about the schema and entity-relation diagrams.
02
Phase 2: Data Acquisition

Phase 2: Data Acquisition, Ingestion & ETL

1
Data Ingestion
Setting up the process of moving data from its source locations to the target locations where analytics operations run.
Source Locations are origins (databases, APIs, sensors, files); Target Locations are destinations (data warehouses, data lakes); the Data Pipeline is the automated ETL system connecting them.
E
Extracting (ETL Step 1)
Pulling raw data from single or multiple data sources.
T
Transforming (ETL Step 2)
Transforming data as per business logic via a two-step process: data cleansing and data manipulation.
L
Loading (ETL Step 3)
Loading the transformed data into the target data source or data warehouse for analysis.
Deliverables (Data Acquisition and Understanding)
1
Data Quality Report
Includes data summaries, relationships between attributes and target, and variable ranking.
2
Solution Architecture
A diagram or description of your data pipeline.
3
A Checkpoint Decision
Used to determine if the expected value is sufficient to continue pursuing the project.
03
Phase 3: Data Modeling

Phase 3: Data Modeling & Feature Engineering

1
Model Design
Define the mathematical or statistical approach that best fits your goals (e.g., regression, classification, clustering).
2
Integration of Prior Knowledge
Use everything learned from earlier stages (data sources, dictionaries, requirements) to guide how you structure and relate data entities.
3
Iteration and Refinement
Adjust the model as new insights emerge to improve accuracy and relevance.
Deliverables (Data Modeling)
1
Data Model Diagram
Visual representation of entities, relationships, and data flow.
2
Model Specification Document
Defines algorithms, parameters, and expected outputs.
3
Validation Report
Shows how the model performs against test data and evaluation metrics.
Feature Engineering Activities & Transformations
1
Feature Engineering & Inclusion/Aggregation
Transforming raw data into meaningful inputs (features); Inclusion and Aggregation combines raw variables or summarizes data into new features.
2
Log Transform
Reduces skewness and compresses large values (useful for right-skewed income or population data).
3
Square-Root Transformation
Stabilizes variance and reduces the impact of outliers (often applied to count data like clicks).
4
Categorical Encoding, Power Functions & Scaling
Categorical Encoding converts text categories into numbers (one-hot/label encoding); Power Functions adjust distributions with exponents; Scaling standardizes ranges (Min-Max or Z-score).
5
Skewed Data, Bias Mitigation & Binning
Skewed Data is adjusted for symmetry; Bias Mitigation prevents unfair group favoritism; Binning groups continuous values into discrete intervals.
04
Outliers & Feature Selection

Outlier Treatment, Feature Selection & Phase 4: Deployment

Five Common Outlier Treatment Methods
1
Trimming
Removes extreme values beyond a defined threshold (e.g., top and bottom 1%) when outliers are clearly errors or irrelevant.
2
Winsorizing
Replaces extreme values with the nearest acceptable boundary (e.g., capping at the 95th percentile) to keep all data points while reducing extreme influence.
3
Imputation
Substitutes outlier or missing values with estimated ones (mean, median, or model-based) for continuity.
4
Transformation
Applies mathematical functions (log, square-root) when outliers are legitimate but need normalization.
5
Segmentation
Separates outliers into a different group for specialized analysis when they represent meaningful anomalies (such as fraud detection).
Three Feature Selection Methods
1
Filter-Based Method
Selects features using statistical metrics without involving a predictive model (fast and scalable).
Examples: Correlation (removes redundant correlated features), Chi-square Test (dependence between categorical variables), and ANOVA (tests if group means differ significantly).
2
Wrapper-Based Method
Treats feature selection as a search problem by training models on feature subsets and evaluating performance (more accurate, computationally expensive).
Examples: Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), Forward Selection (starts empty, adds features), and Backward Selection (starts full, removes features).
3
Embedded Method
Uses algorithms with built-in feature selection during model training.
Examples: LASSO / L1 Regularization (shrinks coefficients and eliminates less important features) and Ridge / L2 Regularization (reduces coefficient size while keeping all features to control overfitting).
Phase 4: Deployment Activities & Deliverables
1
Model Operationalization, UAT & API Exposure
Convert the model into a deployable container/API, validate with end-users in User Acceptance Testing (UAT), and expose via an open API for websites, dashboards, spreadsheets, and backend apps.
2
Phase 4 Deliverables
Status Dashboard (system health, uptime, metrics), Final Modeling Report (performance and validation summary), and Solution Architecture Document (technical setup and integration points).

Lesson 4: Statistical Inference, Measures & Probability Distributions

01
Foundations of Inference

Statistical Inference, Descriptive Stats & Basic Terminologies

What is Statistical Inference?
Combines mathematics, empirical science, and logical reasoning to use data from a sample to estimate, predict, and draw conclusions about a larger population.
Flow: 1. Extract Sample from Population → 2. Perform Statistical Analysis / Modelling → 3. Parameter Estimation back to Population.

Descriptive Statistics

1
Describes Collected Data
Summarizes and presents collected data using tables, charts, graphs, and measures like mean, median, and mode.
Example: Calculating the average score of 30 students in a class.
VS

Statistical Inference

2
Generalizes to a Population
Uses sample data to make estimates or draw conclusions about a larger population (via Regression, Confidence Intervals, and Hypothesis Tests).
Example: Using the scores of 30 students to estimate the average performance of all students in a college.
Basic Statistical Terminologies (With 1,000 College Students Example)
1
Population
The entire group of individuals or objects being studied.
Example: All 1,000 students in the college.
2
Parameter
A numerical value describing a characteristic of a population (such as population mean μ or population proportion).
Example: The true average grade of all 1,000 college students.
3
Sample
A subset of a population selected for study (used because studying an entire population is costly and time-consuming).
Example: 100 selected students surveyed from the college.
4
Statistic
A numerical value describing a sample (such as sample mean x̄ or sample standard deviation s), used to estimate or test a population parameter.
Example: The average grade of the 100 selected students.
02
Central Tendency

Measures of Center: Mean, Frequency Mean, Median & Mode

1
Mean (Average) — Individual Data Formula & Example
Found by adding all numbers in the dataset and dividing by the number of values n. Population mean uses Greek letter μ; sample mean uses x̄.
Limitation: Extremely high or low outliers can significantly distort the mean.
Formula: x̄ = (Σx) / n Example (Dataset: 1, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5): x̄ = (1 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 5 + 5) / 8 = 29 / 8 = 3.625 (shown as 3.652 in slide box)
2
Mean with Frequency Distribution — Formula & Worked Table
When data are summarized by frequencies f, multiply each value x by its frequency f (xf), sum the xf column, and divide by total count n.
Formula: x̄ = (Σ(x · f)) / n

Median (Middle Value) — Formula & Example
The middle value when a dataset is ordered from least to greatest. If n is even, average the two middle values.
Advantage: One extreme outlier does not impact the median as significantly as the mean, making it more stable on skewed data.

03
Variability & Dispersion

Measures of Spread: Range, Interquartile Range (IQR) & Standard Deviation

Why Measures of Spread Matter (Same Center, Different Spread)
Datasets can share the exact same Mean (5) and Median (5) while having completely different spreads: 1, 1, 1, 5, 9, 9, 9 (spread far apart), 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 (moderately spread), and 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5 (zero spread).
1
Range — Formula, Examples & Limitation
Measures the difference between the largest and smallest values in a dataset.
Limitation: A single extreme outlier greatly inflates the range (e.g., if min is 1 and max is 27, Range = 27 − 1 = 26).
Formula: Range = Maximum Value − Minimum Value Example 1: For 3, 5, 8, 10 → Range = 10 − 3 = 7 Example 2: For 1, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5 → Range = 5 − 1 = 4
2
Interquartile Range (IQR) — Formula & Worked Example
Measures the range of the middle 50% of the data by subtracting the first quartile (Q1) from the third quartile (Q3).
Advantage: Not impacted by extreme outliers. Limitation: Only considers two values (Q1 and Q3) rather than all observations.
Formula: IQR = Q3 − Q1 Example: 1, 3, | 3, 4, | 4, 4, | 5, 5 Q1 = (3 + 3)/2 = 3, Median = 4, Q3 = (4 + 5)/2 = 4.5 IQR = 4.5 − 3 = 1.5
3
Sample Standard Deviation ( s ) — Individual Data Formula & Worked Example
The most important measure of spread: measures the average distance of all data values from the mean.
We square each deviation (x − x̄)2 so positive and negative differences do not cancel out, divide by n − 1, and take the square root.
Formula: s = √[ Σ(x − x̄)2 / (n − 1) ] Worked Example (Given data: 11, 13, 14, 14): Step 1 (Mean): x̄ = (11 + 13 + 14 + 14) / 4 = 52 / 4 = 13

04
Z-Scores & Outliers

Z-Score Formulas & Two Methods for Calculating Outliers

1
Z-Score (Number of Standard Deviations from the Mean)
The variable Z represents the number of standard deviations a value x is from the mean.
Formulas: 1) Find Z from x: z = (x − x̄) / s 2) Find x from Z: x = x̄ + z · s Worked Example A (Dataset: 1, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5 where median = 4, x̄ = 3.625, s = 1.3): How many standard deviations from the mean is the median? Z = (4 − 3.625) / 1.3 = 0.288 standard deviations from the mean Worked Example B (Same data): What value is 2 standard deviations BELOW the mean (z = −2)? x = 3.625 − 2(1.3) = 1.025
2
Outliers Definition
Values far removed from the rest of the data (either unusually large or unusually small, such as 87 in 1, 3, 3, 5, 87).
Since 95% of data generally falls within 2 standard deviations of the mean, values beyond 2 SDs are considered unusual or extreme outliers.
3
Outlier Method 1 — IQR Method ( 1.5 × IQR Rule )
A value cannot be more than 1.5 × IQR below Q1 or above Q3. Any value outside those cutoffs is an outlier.
Cutoffs: Below: Q1 − 1.5 × IQR Above: Q3 + 1.5 × IQR Worked Example (Dataset: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 14): Q1 = 3, Median = 5.5, Q3 = 7 → IQR = 7 − 3 = 4 Below Cutoff = 3 − 1.5(4) = −3 (no values below −3) Above Cutoff = 7 + 1.5(4) = 13 (14 > 13, so 14 is an outlier)
4
Outlier Method 2 — Standard Deviation Method ( ± 2s Rule )
Any value more than two standard deviations below or above the mean is classified as an outlier.
Cutoffs: Below: x̄ − 2s Above: x̄ + 2s Worked Example (Dataset: 1, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5 where x̄ = 3.625, s = 1.3): Below Cutoff = 3.625 − 2(1.3) = 1.025 (value 1 is below 1.025) Above Cutoff = 3.625 + 2(1.3) = 6.225

Probability & Bayes

Probability in Data Science, Bayes’ Theorem & Event Independence

Probability (Future Likelihood)

1
Predicts What Might Happen
Predicts the likelihood or chance that an event will happen in the future over repeated trials.
Example: Predicting the chance of rain tomorrow.
VS

Statistics (Past Frequency)

2
Analyzes What Has Happened
Analyzes observed data and the frequency of events that have already occurred.
Example: Analyzing how often it rained during the past month.
Four Applications of Probability in Data Science
1
Statistical Inference
Determines how accurately sample survey results reflect population preferences to guide marketing and policy.
2
Machine Learning
Powers classification models like Naive Bayes, regression, and decision trees to predict class likelihoods under uncertainty.
3
Bayesian Analysis
Combines prior knowledge (Prior beliefs) with new data (Evidence) to update the probability of an outcome (Posterior beliefs).
Example: A doctor updates the probability that a patient has a disease as new test results arrive.
4
Risk Assessment
Estimates the likelihood of unwanted events to reduce losses across Finance (loan defaults), Healthcare (disease outbreaks), and Insurance (policyholder risk).
Probability Terminologies, Formulas & Worked Examples
1
Event ( E ), Trial & Random Variable
An Event (E) is a set of outcomes to which a probability P(E) is assigned; a Trial is a situation where E might succeed or fail; a Random Variable represents the outcome of events (probabilities range from 0 to 1).
2
Empirical (Practical) Probability
The number of times the event occurs (s) divided by total observed trials (n).
Formula: P = s / n Example (Toss a coin 4 times → H, H, H, T): P(Head) = 3 / 4 = 0.75 P(Tail) = 1 / 4 = 0.25
3
Theoretical (Classical) Probability & Worked Examples
The number of favorable outcomes n(E) divided by the total number of possible outcomes in the sample space n(S).
Formula: P(E) = n(E) / n(S) Worked Example 1 (Roll a 6-sided die for an even number): Sample Space S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} → n(S) = 6 Event E (even) = {2, 4, 6} → n(E) = 3 P(E) = 3 / 6 = 1 / 2 = 0.5 (50%) Worked Example 2 (Toss two coins for two heads): Sample Space S = {(H,T), (H,H), (T,H), (T,T)} → n(S) = 4 Event E (two heads) = {(H,H)} → n(E) = 1 P(E) = 1 / 4 = 0.25 (25%)
4
Joint Probability ( P(A ∩ B) )
The probability that events A and B both occur. If A and B are independent, multiply their individual probabilities.
Independent Events: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) Dependent Events: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A)
5
Conditional Probability ( P(A | B) )
The probability of event A given that event B has already occurred (used when events are not independent).
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A)
6
Bayes’ Theorem
Calculates the Posterior Probability P(H | E) of hypothesis H given evidence E.
P(H | E) = [ P(H) · P(E | H) ] / P(E) Where: • P(H | E) = Posterior probability of Hypothesis H given Evidence E • P(H) = Prior probability of Hypothesis H • P(E | H) = Likelihood of Evidence E if Hypothesis H is true • P(E) = Prior probability that Evidence E itself is true
7
Independent, Dependent, Pairwise & Mutually Independent Events
Independent: One event does not affect the other (e.g., red die even P(A)=1/2 and blue die odd P(B)=1/2). Pairwise Independent: Every pair is independent. Mutually Independent: Every event is independent of every intersection of the other events.
Two-Dice Example (A: red=3, B: blue=4, C: sum=7): • Pairwise check: P(A|B) = 1/6, P(A|C) = 1/6, P(B|C) = 1/6 → Pairwise Independent • Mutual check: P(A | (B ∩ C)) = 1 vs. P(A | (B ∩ C)′) = 1/7 Since 1 ≠ 1/7, events A, B, and C are Mutually Dependent (NOT mutually independent).
06
Probability Distributions

Discrete, Normal (68–95–99.7), Standard Normal (Z) & Binomial Distributions

1
Probability Distribution (Discrete, Univariate, Finite Support)
Lists all possible outcomes of a random variable along with their probability values (e.g., fair 6-sided die: outcomes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 each with probability 1/6).
Discrete: No outcomes exist in between consecutive values (cannot roll 1.5) • Univariate: Only one random variable • Finite Support: Limited set of defined outcomes {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
2
Normal Distribution (Gaussian Distribution / Bell Curve) & Empirical Rule
A symmetric bell-shaped distribution where 50% of data lie on each side and Mean = Median = Mode at the exact center.
Fits natural phenomena like heights, blood pressure, measurement error, and IQ scores.
Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7): • 68% of data falls within 1 SD (±1σ) of the mean • 95% of data falls within 2 SDs (±2σ) of the mean • 99.7% of data falls within 3 SDs (±3σ) of the mean Worked Example 1 (Men’s Height: μ = 178 cm, σ = 7 cm): 68% of population are between 178 − 7 = 171 cm and 178 + 7 = 185 cm. Worked Example 2 (95% of students are between 1.1m and 1.7m tall): • Mean = halfway between 1.1m and 1.7m = (1.1 + 1.7) / 2 = 1.4m • 95% spans 2 SDs on each side (total of 4 SDs): 1 Standard Deviation = (1.7m − 1.1m) / 4 = 0.6m / 4 = 0.15m

Standard Normal Z-Distribution & Apples-to-Oranges Comparison
Standardizes any normal distribution so it always has Mean (μ) = 0 and Standard Deviation (σ) = 1.
Formula: z = (X − μ) / σ Worked Example 1 (Test Score X = 190, μ = 150, σ = 25): z = (190 − 150) / 25 = 40 / 25 = +1.6 (1.6 SDs above the mean) Worked Example 2 (Comparing Apple [110g] vs. Orange [100g]): • Apples (μ = 100g, σ = 15g): z = (110 − 100) / 15 = +0.667 (above average apple) • Oranges (μ = 140g, σ = 25g): z = (100 − 140) / 25 = −1.600 (well below average orange)

Binomial Distribution & Bernoulli Distribution
Measures the probability of getting exactly x successes in n independent trials where each trial has two outcomes (Success p or Failure q = 1 − p).
Note: A Bernoulli distribution is simply a Binomial distribution with a single trial (n = 1).
Formula 1 (Combinations Form): P(x) = nCx · px · (1 − p)n−x Formula 2 (Factorials Form): P(X) = [ n! / ((n − X)! · X!) ] · pX · qn−X Worked Example (A coin is tossed n = 10 times; find probability of x = 6 heads): • Given: n = 10, x = 6, p = 0.5, q = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 • Combinations: 10C6 = 10! / ((10 − 6)! · 6!) = 10! / (4! · 6!) = 210 • Probability: P(x = 6) = 210 × (0.5)6 × (0.5)4 = 210 × 0.015625 × 0.0625 = 0.205078125 (~20.51%)
