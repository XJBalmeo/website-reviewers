Human-Computer Interaction

Lesson 1: Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)

01
Overview

What is Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)?

Core Principle: “We must design for the way people behave, not for how we would wish them to behave.”
1
Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)
Is the study of how people interact with computers and to what extent computers are or are not developed for successful interaction with human beings.
It bridges human behavior and technology so machines adapt to people instead of forcing people to adapt to machines.
02
Core Goals

Goals of HCI

1
Understand the factors that determine how people use technology
Identify the human, social, and environmental factors that drive or block technology adoption.
Designers study user habits, environments, and cognitive limits before building a system.
2
Develop tools and techniques to enable building suitable systems
Create methods, guidelines, and frameworks for building user-friendly hardware and software.
This gives developers reliable prototyping and evaluation tools to build better interfaces.
3
Achieve efficient, effective, and safe interaction
Ensure tasks are fast (efficient), accurate (effective), and error-free (safe).
Users can finish their goals quickly without making costly or dangerous mistakes.
4
Put people first
Prioritize human needs, abilities, and comfort above machine convenience.
Every design choice is judged by how well it serves the human user.
03
Importance

Importance of HCI

1
Improved Usability
Ensures systems are easy to learn and use.
New users can operate the software right away without needing complex manuals.
2
Enhanced Efficiency
Helps users accomplish tasks faster and more accurately.
Streamlined steps save time and reduce daily workload effort.
3
Safety
Reduces errors and risks, especially in critical systems.
Prevents dangerous mistakes in medical, aviation, or financial software.
4
User Engagement
Encourages positive interaction and continued use.
Enjoyable interfaces keep users interested and coming back to the product.
5
Innovation
Enables new and better ways of interacting with technology.
Drives breakthroughs like touchscreens, voice control, and gesture interfaces.
6
Ethical Design
Promotes fairness, accessibility, and responsible technology use.
Protects user privacy and ensures nobody is excluded or tricked by the interface.
7
Adaptability
Allows systems to meet the needs of diverse users and contexts.
The software adjusts smoothly across different devices, environments, and user skill levels.
04
System Quality

Usability & Usability Importance

Usability (A Usable System Is)
1
Easy to learn (Learnability)
Beginners can figure out how to use the system quickly on their first try.
Clear buttons and layouts let new users start working without formal training.
2
Easy to remember how to use (Memorability)
Users do not forget how it works after taking a break from using it.
When returning weeks later, users can operate the interface without relearning it.
3
Effective to use (Effectiveness)
Allows users to complete their goals accurately and completely.
The system produces the exact result the user intended to achieve.
4
Efficient to use (Efficiency)
Tasks can be finished quickly with minimal effort once learned.
It avoids unnecessary clicks or repetitive steps that waste the user’s time.
5
Safe to use (Safety)
Protects users from making severe mistakes and lets them recover easily.
Includes confirmation prompts and an Undo button to prevent accidental data loss.
6
Enjoyable to use (Satisfaction)
Using the system feels pleasant, comfortable, and stress-free.
A smooth experience leaves the user feeling satisfied rather than frustrated.
Usability Importance
1
Improves User Experience
Users interact easily and with less frustration.
Smooth navigation removes confusion and annoyance during daily use.
2
Enhances Efficiency
Tasks are completed faster and with less effort.
Users spend less mental energy figuring out the screen and more energy finishing work.
3
Reduces Errors
Clear and intuitive design minimizes user mistakes.
Well-labeled controls stop users from clicking the wrong action by accident.
4
Increases Accessibility
More users, including those with disabilities, can use the system.
Inclusive design makes software usable for people with visual, motor, or hearing impairments.
5
Boosts Adoption
Users are more likely to accept and continue using the system.
People naturally stick with tools that make their lives easier instead of harder.
05
System Context

Factors in HCI

1
Organization Factors
The rules, policies, culture, and structure of an organization where the system is used.
Company policies control who is allowed to access features and what workflows employees must follow.
2
Environmental Factors
The physical surroundings where interaction happens (lighting, noise, temperature, space).
An app designed for a quiet office may fail outdoors in bright sunlight or on a loud factory floor.
3
The User
The people who use the system (age, skills, experience, physical and cognitive abilities, culture).
Systems must be tailored to match the actual capabilities and limits of their target users.
4
Comfort Factors
Physical ease and ergonomics while using a system (posture, screen position, keyboard/mouse design).
Poor ergonomics cause fatigue, stress, or physical injury that lowers user performance.
5
User Interface (UI)
The part of the system users directly interact with (screens, buttons, menus, icons, gestures).
A clear, consistent interface lets users complete tasks easily with fewer mistakes.
6
Task Factors
The type of work users need to accomplish (task complexity, frequency, and time pressure).
Understanding the task helps designers structure steps so work gets done accurately.
7
Constraints
Limitations affecting system design and usage (budget, time, legal rules, technical or hardware limits).
Good HCI design works within project budgets and hardware limits while still prioritizing usability.
8
System Functionality
What the system can actually do—its features, operations, and capabilities.
It must provide the exact features needed for user tasks without cluttering the screen with useless extras.
9
Productivity Factors
How the system helps users perform tasks faster, better, and with less effort.
A well-designed tool boosts output quality and helps users reach their goals efficiently.
06
Disciplines

Disciplines Contributing to HCI

1
Computer Science
Provides the software, code, and technical architecture behind interactive systems.
Covers programming languages, algorithms, operating systems, and user interface toolkits.
2
Cognitive Psychology
Studies internal mental processes like memory, perception, attention, and learning.
Helps designers build screens that match how the human brain naturally thinks and remembers.
3
Social Psychology
Studies how people behave and interact in social situations.
Explains how users communicate with each other inside chat apps, social media, and team tools.
4
Ergonomics / Human Factors
Designs hardware around the physical body to ensure comfort and safety.
Shapes physical devices like keyboards, chairs, and VR headsets to prevent muscle strain.
5
Linguistics
Studies the structure and meaning of human language.
Used to write clear button labels and build voice assistants or natural language chatbots.
6
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Simulates human intelligence to create smart, adaptive interfaces.
Powers features like voice recognition, predictive text, and personalized recommendations.
7
Philosophy, Sociology, and Anthropology
Examines ethics and logic (Philosophy), group behavior (Sociology), and human cultures and societies (Anthropology).
Guides ethical tech design, workplace group dynamics, and field-observation of users in their real cultural environments.
8
Engineering and Design
Combines technical engineering with visual and industrial product design.
Turns abstract ideas into tangible, functional, and visually appealing hardware and software.

Lesson 2: Psychology of Everyday Things

01
Gulfs & Parts of Action

The Gulfs of Execution & Evaluation and Two Parts to an Action

Role of the Designer: Help people bridge the two gulfs so they never need another person to explain how a machine works.

Gulf of Execution

1
Gulf of Execution
Where users try to figure out how it operates.
It is the gap between the user’s goal and the controls available to perform the action.
2
Execution Questions
Asks “How do I work this?” and “What can I do?”
Bridged by clear affordances, signifiers, constraints, and natural mappings.
VS

Gulf of Evaluation

1
Gulf of Evaluation
Where users try to figure out what happened.
It is the gap between the system’s new state and the user’s ability to tell if their goal succeeded.
2
Evaluation Questions
Asks “What happened?” and “Is this what I wanted?”
Bridged by immediate feedback and a clear conceptual model.
Two Parts to an Action
1
Executing the action (Doing)
Planning, specifying, and physically performing the action upon the world.
Corresponds to bridging the Gulf of Execution.
2
Evaluating the results (Interpreting)
Perceiving, interpreting, and comparing what happened against the original goal.
Corresponds to bridging the Gulf of Evaluation.
02
Action Cycle

The Seven Stages of Action

1
Goal (form the goal)
Decide what you want to achieve.
You set an intention in your mind before taking any action.
2
Plan (the action)
Think of a general way to reach your goal (Bridge of Execution).
You choose which tool, approach, or path to use.
3
Specify (an action sequence)
Pick the exact steps or buttons to press (Bridge of Execution).
You mentally map out the exact sequence of taps or clicks required.
4
Perform (the action sequence)
Physically execute the action sequence with your body (Bridge of Execution).
You tap the buttons or move the controls to act upon the world.
5
Perceive (the state of the world)
See or hear what changed in the world after your action (Bridge of Evaluation).
Your senses detect a new visual message, color change, or sound on the device.
6
Interpret (the perception)
Figure out what that perceived change means (Bridge of Evaluation).
Your brain translates the sensory cue into meaning.
7
Compare (the outcome with the goal)
Check whether the final outcome matches your original goal (Bridge of Evaluation).
You decide if your goal is complete or if further subgoals and actions are needed.
03
Study of Mind & Overlearning

Study of Mind Disciplines & Overlearning

Study of Mind Disciplines
1
Behavioral and Social Sciences
Examine observable human actions, habits, and social interactions.
Helps explain how people act individually and in groups when using everyday tools.
2
Cognitive Science
Studies how the mind processes information, solves problems, and forms mental models.
Explains attention, perception, reasoning, and decision-making.
3
Neuroscience
Investigates the biological brain and nervous system structures behind thought and emotion.
Reveals how subconscious and conscious brain pathways respond to stimuli.
4
Philosophy
Explores the nature of consciousness, knowledge, logic, and human reasoning.
Provides foundational questions about how the mind understands reality.
5
Information and Computer Sciences
Model information processing, representation, and computation in both humans and machines.
Connects human cognitive workflows with digital system architectures.
Subconscious Thought & Overlearning
1
Overlearning
Produced after the initial learning, continued practice and study, sometimes for thousands of hours over a period of years.
Once overlearned, performance becomes effortless and automatic with little or no conscious awareness.
2
Subconscious vs. Conscious Attention
Most brain operations are subconscious; conscious attention is needed mainly when initially learning new tasks or handling unexpected problems.
Only the highest level of processing (the reflective level) is conscious.
04
Memory Systems

Declarative Memory vs. Procedural Memory

Declarative Memory

1
Declarative Memory
Memory for factual information.
Used when consciously recalling facts, names, dates, or written rules.
VS

Procedural Memory

2
Procedural Memory
Recalling the activities performed (motor skills and physical actions).
Used when remembering how to ride a bike, type on a keyboard, or reach for a doorknob.
05
Levels of Processing

Levels of Processing

1. Visceral
The most basic level of processing; sometimes referred to as the “lizard brain”.
Subconscious and immediate—produces fast, automatic gut reactions to how a product looks, feels, and sounds.
2. Behavioral
Home of learned skills, triggered by situations that match the appropriate terms or patterns.
Subconscious and action-oriented—focuses on how well the product works while performing tasks.
3. Reflective
Home of conscious cognition.
Conscious and analytical—where deep understanding develops, reasoning occurs, and users judge pride, value, and meaning.

Lesson 3: Interaction Design

01
Design Quality

Characteristics of Bad Design vs. Good Design

Core Metaphor: Bad design is like smoke (it misleads the user and obscures the right direction), whereas good design is like a mirror (it is reflective and displays the clear truth).

Characteristics of Bad Design

1
Cluttered interface
Overcrowded screen packed with too many competing elements.
2
Poor navigation
Hard to find menus or move smoothly between pages.
3
Confusing layout
Disorganized placement of buttons, controls, and content.
4
Complex tasks
Simple user goals require too many difficult or unnecessary steps.
5
Irrelevant user feedback
Unhelpful, vague, or confusing system alerts after an action.
6
Inconsistent experience
Controls change look or behavior unpredictably across screens.
7
Difficult content
Hard-to-read text or confusing technical jargon.
8
Unpleasant color scheme
Harsh, clashing colors that strain the eyes and hurt readability.
VS

Characteristics of Good Design

1
Transparent and visible to users
Available actions and system status are clearly seen at all times.
2
Simple
Clean, minimal, and focused only on what matters to the user.
3
Relevant
Information and features directly match the user’s current need.
4
Usable
Easy and effortless for target users to operate.
5
Problem solver
Directly resolves the user’s actual task or issue.
6
Easy to understand
Self-explanatory without needing complex instructions.
7
Effective to meet the goals
Reliably achieves the desired outcome accurately.
8
Fresh with pleasant color scheme
Visually balanced, modern, and comfortable to view.
02
Definition & Goal

Interaction Design & Goal of Interaction Design

1
Interaction Design (IxD)
Designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives.
For UX designers, it is the axis on which their work revolves, and the term also applies to understanding how people interact with non-digital products.
2
John Kolko’s Definition (Thoughts on Interaction Design)
According to John Kolko, author of Thoughts on Interaction Design: “Interaction Design is the creation of a dialogue between a person and a product, system, or service.”
This dialogue is both physical and emotional in nature and is manifested in the interplay between form, function, and technology as experienced over time.
Goal of Interaction Design
1
Useful
Solves a real user need and provides meaningful utility.
2
Usable
Can be operated easily, intuitively, and without confusion.
3
Satisfying
Creates a pleasant, engaging, and rewarding experience for the user.
03
1D to 5D Framework

Five Dimensions of Interactive Design

1. Words (1D)
Encompass text, such as button labels, which help give users the right amount of information.
Words should be meaningful and simple to understand without overwhelming the user.
2. Visual Representations (2D)
Are graphical elements such as images, typography, and icons that aid in user interaction.
Supplement the words to communicate information quickly at a glance.
3. Physical Objects / Space (3D)
Refers to the medium through which users interact with the product or service.
For instance, interacting with a laptop via a physical mouse, or a mobile phone via fingers in a real environment.
4. Time (4D)
Relates to the media that changes with time, such as animations, videos, and sounds.
Also includes the duration a user spends interacting with the product and tracking progress.
5. Behavior (5D)
Is concerned with how the previous four dimensions define the interactions a product affords, and how the product reacts to the user’s inputs and provides feedback.
For instance, how users perform actions on a website or operate a car, and how the system responds.
04
Core Components

Core Components of Interaction Design

1
Users
Interaction design starts by understanding who will use the system.
Designers study user goals, skills, experiences, and limitations.
2
Tasks
Is what users want to accomplish.
Interfaces are organized around the specific steps needed to finish these activities.
3
Context of Use
Refers to where and how the system will be used.
Covers the physical environment, social setting, and hardware conditions during use.
05
Design Principles

Key Principles of Interaction Design

1
Visibility
Users would easily see what actions are possible.
Important controls and options are placed in plain sight instead of hidden away.
2
Feedback
The system should inform users about what is happening after an action.
Visual highlights, progress bars, or sounds confirm that an input was received.
3
Consistency
Similar actions should behave in the same way throughout the system.
Uniform icons, colors, and layouts make the interface predictable and easy to learn.
4
Constraints
Limits prevent users from making mistakes.
Examples include disabling a Submit button until all required form fields are completed.
5
Affordances
Design elements should suggest how they are used.
A raised button visually suggests pushing or clicking, while a slider track suggests dragging.
06
Interaction Styles

Types of Interaction

1
Command-Based Interaction
Users interact by typing text commands directly into a command line or terminal.
Fast and powerful for expert users, though it requires memorizing exact command syntax.
2
Menu-Based Interaction
Users choose actions from a predefined list or hierarchy of menus presented on screen.
Reduces memory load because users recognize options instead of recalling commands from scratch (e.g., ATMs, restaurant kiosks).
3
Graphical User Interface (GUI)
Users interact with visual elements like Windows, Icons, Menus, and Pointers (WIMP).
Allows direct manipulation of visual objects on desktop and web applications using a mouse or trackpad.
4
Touch Interaction
Users interact directly with the screen using fingers or a stylus via taps, swipes, and pinch gestures.
Standard on smartphones, tablets, and interactive public displays.
5
Voice Interaction
Users communicate with the system using spoken natural language commands.
Ideal for hands-free environments, smart speakers, and accessibility support.
07
Design Process

Interaction Design Process

1
Identify User Needs
Understand the Target, Tasks, Problems, and Environment before designing solutions.
4 Key Focus Areas: Target (who the users are), Tasks (what they do), Problems (pain points to solve), and Environment (where the system is used).
2
Develop Alternative Designs
Create multiple possible design solutions to address the identified user needs.
Explores different layouts, navigation structures, and interaction methods before committing to one.
3
Build Interactive Prototypes
Create early testable versions using Low-Fidelity Prototypes and High-Fidelity Prototypes.
Low-Fidelity Prototypes: quick, simple paper sketches or basic wireframes to test early concepts • High-Fidelity Prototypes: detailed, interactive digital mockups that look and behave like the final product.
4
Design Evaluation
Assess the prototype through Usability Testing, Expert Evaluation, and User Feedback.
Usability Testing: real users perform tasks on the prototype • Expert Evaluation: specialists inspect the interface against heuristics • User Feedback: comments and suggestions from users.
5
Iteration and Refinement
Revise and improve the system repeatedly based on evaluation findings.
Teams repeat testing and fixing until the product meets usability goals.

Lesson 4: User-Centered Design

01
UCD & HCD

User-Centered Design (UCD) & Human-Centered Design (HCD)

1
User-Centered Design (UCD)
A collection of processes that focus on putting users at the center of product design and development.
It is a process that involves the users of a product throughout its lifecycle, from planning through post-release assessment.
2
Human-Centered Design (HCD)
An approach that focuses on fully understanding the perspectives of the people the design is for in each step of the process.
While HCD looks broadly at human perspectives and emotions, UCD zooms in specifically on target end-users and their concrete tasks.
Goal of UCD
1
Useful
Ensures the product is relevant and solves the user’s real needs.
2
Usable
Ensures the product has high ease-of-use so users can achieve their goals effortlessly.
02
Principles & Elements

Important UCD Principles & Essential Elements of UCD

Core Principle: “People ignore design that ignores people.”
Important UCD Principles
1
Involve users from the very beginning
Evaluate critical design decisions based on how they work for end-users right from project kickoff.
Never wait until the end of development to test if users understand the product.
2
User-centered design should be empathetic
Step into the user’s shoes and focus on solving their actual pain points.
Prioritize fixing real user struggles rather than building features based on assumptions.
3
Use an iterative design process
Go through repeated cycles of defining, prototyping, testing, and refining.
New discoveries about users loop back to improve the design continuously.
4
Involve multiple feedback loops
Gather qualitative and quantitative user feedback at multiple stages of development.
Frequent check-ins keep the product aligned with user expectations.
5
Follow the fundamentals of design
Keep things simple and apply core visual hierarchy, contrast, and layout rules.
Clear visual fundamentals make the interface intuitive to scan and operate.
Essential Elements of UCD
1
Visibility
Users should be able to see from the beginning what they can do with the product, what it is about, and how they can use it.
The purpose and primary actions of the interface are immediately obvious.
2
Accessibility
Users should be able to find information easily and quickly.
Supported by clear navigation menus, search bars, and call-to-action buttons.
3
Legibility
Text should be easy to read.
Uses clean typography, comfortable font sizes, and strong contrast.
4
Language
Short sentences are preferred here.
Simple, direct phrasing allows users to understand instructions at a glance.
03
Research Methods

UCD Methods

1
Focus Groups
A moderated group of intended users collectively share thoughts and opinions to define product use cases.
Provides multiple qualitative perspectives at once with a small sample size.
2
Questionnaires and Surveys
Structured written questions used to obtain a large amount of statistical data on user challenges and preferences.
Collects both qualitative and quantitative data from a large sample size at low cost.
3
Interviews
One-on-one open conversations conducted early to dig for in-depth individual insights.
Reveals deep personal motivations and pain points, though time-consuming per participant.
4
Usability Testing
Users interact with a prototype or product directly while a moderator observes and records feedback.
Uncovers concrete interface bottlenecks using both qualitative and quantitative task metrics.
5
Card Sorting
Users sort content cards into labeled groups to design and test the information architecture of a site or app.
Ensures menu categories match how real users naturally group information.
6
Participatory Design
Actively involves end-users as co-designers alongside the product team during design workshops.
Users help sketch and shape solutions directly so the interface fits their real workflow.
04
UCD Process

UCD Process: Research, Concept Ideation & Validation

Stage 1: Research
1
Personas
Archetypes of real users representing a target group with similar behaviors, needs, goals, and skills.
Answers “Who uses our product?” so the team designs for concrete people.
2
Interactions (User environment, Medium, Mood)
Defines how users interact with the product across User environment, Medium, and Mood.
User environment: physical setting where interaction happens • Medium: device or screen used • Mood: user’s emotional state during the task.
Stage 2: Concept Ideation
1
Journey Mapping
A visual timeline that maps how a user interacts with a product step by step from the user’s point of view.
Highlights user actions, touchpoints, pain points, and emotional highs and lows across phases.
Stage 3: Validation
1
Usability Testing
Evaluates design decisions with actual users performing tasks to see what problems they encounter.
Reveals usability flaws early and continuously throughout development.
2
Contextual Inquiries
Observing and interviewing users while they perform tasks in their natural environment.
Participants work in their real setting and talk aloud about what they are doing and why.
