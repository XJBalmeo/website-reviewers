Human-Computer Interaction

Lesson 1: Introduction to Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)

01
Overview

What is Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)?

Core Principle: “We must design for the way people behave, not for how we would wish them to behave.”
1
Human-Computer Interaction (HCI): The study of how people interact with computers and how well computers are developed for successful human use. It bridges human behavior and technology so machines adapt to people instead of forcing people to adapt to machines.
02
Core Goals

Goals of HCI

1
Understand the factors that determine how people use technology: Identify what drives or blocks human technology use. Designers study user habits, environments, and limits before building a system.
2
Develop tools and techniques to enable building suitable systems: Create methods and frameworks for building user-friendly software. This gives developers reliable prototyping and testing tools to build better interfaces.
3
Achieve efficient, effective, and safe interaction: Ensure tasks are fast (efficient), accurate (effective), and error-free (safe). Users can finish their goals quickly without making costly or dangerous mistakes.
4
Put people first: Prioritize human needs and comfort above machine convenience. Every design choice is judged by how well it serves the human user.
03
Importance

Importance of HCI

1
Improved Usability: Ensures systems are easy to learn and use. New users can operate the software right away without needing complex manuals.
2
Enhanced Efficiency: Helps users accomplish tasks faster and more accurately. Streamlined steps save time and reduce daily workload effort.
3
Safety: Reduces errors and risks, especially in critical systems. Prevents dangerous mistakes in medical, aviation, or financial software.
4
User Engagement: Encourages positive interaction and continued use. Enjoyable interfaces keep users interested and coming back to the product.
5
Innovation: Enables new and better ways of interacting with technology. Drives breakthroughs like touchscreens, voice control, and gesture interfaces.
6
Ethical Design: Promotes fairness, accessibility, and responsible technology use. Protects user privacy and ensures nobody is excluded or tricked by the interface.
7
Adaptability: Allows systems to meet the needs of diverse users and contexts. The software adjusts smoothly across different devices, environments, and user skill levels.
04
System Quality

Usability & Why It Is Important

A Usable System Is
1
Easy to learn (Learnability): Beginners can figure out how to use it quickly on their first try. Clear buttons and layouts let new users start working without formal training.
2
Easy to remember how to use (Memorability): Users don’t forget how it works after taking a break from it. When you return weeks later, you can still use the interface without relearning it.
3
Effective to use (Effectiveness): Allows users to complete their goals accurately and completely. The system produces the exact result the user intended to achieve.
4
Efficient to use (Efficiency): Tasks can be finished quickly with minimal effort once learned. It avoids unnecessary clicks or repetitive steps that waste the user’s time.
5
Safe to use (Safety): Protects users from making severe mistakes and lets them recover easily. Includes confirmation prompts and an Undo button to prevent accidental data loss.
6
Enjoyable to use (Satisfaction): Using the system feels pleasant, comfortable, and stress-free. A smooth experience leaves the user feeling happy rather than frustrated.
Why is Usability Important?
1
Improves User Experience: Users interact easily and with less frustration. Smooth navigation removes confusion and annoyance during daily use.
2
Enhances Efficiency: Tasks are completed faster and with less effort. Users spend less mental energy figuring out the screen and more energy finishing work.
3
Reduces Errors: Clear and intuitive design minimizes user mistakes. Well-labeled controls stop users from clicking the wrong action by accident.
4
Increases Accessibility: More users, including those with disabilities, can use the system. Inclusive design makes software usable for people with visual, motor, or hearing impairments.
5
Boosts Adoption: Users are more likely to accept and continue using the system. People naturally stick with tools that make their lives easier instead of harder.
05
System Context

Factors in HCI

1
Organizational Factors: The rules, policies, culture, and structure of an organization where the system is used. Company policies control who is allowed to access features and what workflows employees must follow.
2
Environmental Factors: The physical surroundings where interaction happens (lighting, noise, temperature, space). An app designed for a quiet office may fail outdoors in bright sunlight or on a loud factory floor.
3
The User: The people who use the system (age, skills, experience, physical/cognitive abilities, culture). Systems must be tailored to match the actual capabilities and limits of their target users.
4
Comfort Factors: Physical ease and ergonomics while using a system (posture, screen position, keyboard/mouse design). Poor ergonomics cause fatigue, stress, or physical injury that lowers user performance.
5
User Interface (UI): The part of the system users directly interact with (screens, buttons, menus, icons, gestures). A clear, consistent interface lets users complete tasks easily with fewer mistakes.
6
Task Factors: The type of work users need to accomplish (task complexity, frequency, and time pressure). Understanding the task helps designers structure steps so work gets done accurately.
7
Constraints: Limitations affecting system design and usage (budget, time, legal rules, technical or hardware limits). Good HCI design works within project budgets and hardware limits while still prioritizing usability.
8
System Functionality: What the system can actually do—its features and capabilities. It must provide the exact features needed for user tasks without cluttering the screen with useless extras.
9
Productivity Factors: How the system helps users perform tasks faster, better, and with less effort. A well-designed tool boosts output quality and helps users reach their goals efficiently.
06
Disciplines

Disciplines Contributing to HCI

1
Computer Science: Provides the software, code, and technical architecture behind interactive systems. Covers programming languages, algorithms, operating systems, and user interface toolkits.
2
Cognitive Psychology: Studies internal mental processes like memory, perception, attention, and learning. Helps designers build screens that match how the human brain naturally thinks and remembers.
3
Social Psychology: Studies how people behave in social situations. Explains how users interact with each other inside chat apps, social media, and team tools.
4
Ergonomics / Human Factors: Designs hardware around the physical body to ensure comfort and safety. Shapes physical devices like keyboards, chairs, and VR headsets to prevent muscle strain.
5
Linguistics: Studies the structure and meaning of human language. Used to write clear button labels and build voice assistants or natural language chatbots.
6
Artificial Intelligence (AI): Simulates human intelligence to create smart, adaptive interfaces. Powers features like voice recognition, predictive text, and personalized recommendations.
7
Philosophy: Emphasizes critical thinking, logical reasoning, and ethical reflection. Guides moral decisions in tech, such as protecting user privacy, fairness, and human rights.
8
Sociology: Helps us understand why people behave the way they do in groups. Examines how technology affects teamwork, workplaces, and larger social institutions.
9
Anthropology: Studies who we are, how we live, and how cultures and societies develop over time. Gives HCI field-observation methods to study users in their real everyday cultural environments.
10
Engineering and Design: Combines technical engineering with visual and industrial product design. Turns abstract ideas into tangible, functional, and visually appealing hardware and software.

Lesson 2: The Psychology of Everyday Things

01
Gulfs of Action

The Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation

Role of the Designer: Help people bridge the two gulfs so they never need another person to explain how a machine works.

Gulf of Execution (Doing)

1
Definition: Where users try to figure out how it operates. It is the gap between the user’s goal and the controls available to perform the action.
2
User Questions: “How do I work this?” and “What can I do?” Example: A customer at a fast-food kiosk gets confused by cluttered menus and selects the wrong meal twice.
VS

Gulf of Evaluation (Checking)

1
Definition: Where users try to figure out what happened. It is the gap between the system’s new state and the user’s ability to tell if their goal succeeded.
2
User Questions: “What happened?” and “Is this what I wanted?” Example: The kiosk asks for payment without showing an order summary, leaving the customer unsure what they ordered.
02
Action Cycle

The Seven Stages of Action

1
Goal (Form the goal): Decide what you want to achieve. You set an intention in your mind before taking any action (e.g., “I want to order lunch”).
2
Plan (the action): Think of a general way to reach your goal (Bridge of Execution). You choose which tool or path to use (e.g., deciding to use the self-service kiosk).
3
Specify (an action sequence): Pick the exact steps or buttons to press (Bridge of Execution). You mentally map out the exact taps needed on the screen.
4
Perform (the action sequence): Physically do the steps with your body (Bridge of Execution). You tap the buttons or click the mouse to act upon the world.
5
Perceive (the state of the world): See or hear what changed after your action (Bridge of Evaluation). Your eyes or ears detect a new message, color change, or sound on the device.
6
Interpret (the perception): Figure out what that change means (Bridge of Evaluation). Your brain translates the visual cue into meaning (e.g., “A checkmark means my item was added”).
7
Compare (the outcome with the goal): Check if the result matches your original goal (Bridge of Evaluation). You decide if your task is finished or if you need to take another action.
Characteristics of the Action Cycle
1
Two Parts of an Action: Executing (Doing) and Evaluating (Interpreting). Both require understanding how the item works and what results it produces.
2
Subgoals & Subplans: Complex activities are not satisfied by a single action; goals lead to subgoals and plans lead to subplans. Most behaviors don’t go through all stages in rigid order and use multiple feedback loops over hours or days.
3
Opportunity for Product Enhancement: Detecting where users struggle in either gulf is the starting point for product innovation. Most innovation is done as an incremental enhancement of existing products by observing users.
03
Cognition

Human Thought: Subconscious Mind & Overlearning

1
Why We Study the Mind: Without deep understanding of people, designs become faulty, difficult to use, and difficult to understand. Because things are built for humans, interfaces must fit how the human brain actually works.
2
Disciplines Studying the Mind: Studied across Behavioral and Social Sciences, Cognitive Science, Neuroscience, Philosophy, and Information and Computer Sciences. The human mind is immensely complex, having evolved over a long period with many specialized structures.
3
Mostly Subconscious Operations: Most of the brain’s operations are subconscious, hidden beneath our awareness. Only the highest level of processing, called the reflective level, is conscious.
4
Conscious Attention: Required when initially learning most new things. Beginners must focus carefully on every single step before a skill becomes a habit.
5
Overlearning: Continued practice and study (sometimes for thousands of hours over years) until performance becomes effortless and automatic. Once overlearned, skills are performed automatically with little or no conscious awareness.
04
Memory Systems

Declarative Memory vs. Procedural Memory

Declarative Memory

1
Definition: Memory for factual information. Used when recalling facts like a friend’s phone number or the capital of a country.
VS

Procedural Memory

1
Definition: Memory for activities and motor actions performed. Used when remembering which side an old doorknob was on by mentally picturing yourself reaching for the door.
05
Levels of Processing

Human Cognition & Emotion: The Three Levels of Processing

1. Visceral Level (“Lizard Brain” • Subconscious): The most basic level of processing focused on how it looks and feels (first impression). Just like a lizard reacting instantly to movement, humans have fast, automatic reactions to visual appearance.
2. Behavioral Level (Doing Tasks • Subconscious): The home of learned skills and interaction focused on how it works. It triggers expectation-based emotions like hope and joy when tasks go smoothly, or frustration and anger when they fail.
3. Reflective Level (Conscious Cognition • Conscious): The home of conscious cognition focused on what it means to the user (thinking and evaluation). This is the only conscious level, where users judge a product’s value, pride of ownership, and overall reputation.
1
Understanding vs. Enjoyment: Understanding combines the Behavioral and Reflective levels, while Enjoyment requires all three levels (Visceral, Behavioral, and Reflective) working together. All three levels work in concert to determine a person’s like or dislike of a product or service.
2
Impact of Extreme Experiences: One nasty experience can spoil all future experiences, while one superb experience can make up for past deficiencies. Strong emotional moments leave lasting impressions in the user’s reflective memory.

Lesson 3: Good vs. Bad Design & Interaction Design

01
Design Quality

Good vs. Bad Design

Core Metaphor: Bad design is like smoke (it misleads the user and obscures the right direction), whereas good design is like a mirror (it is reflective and displays the clear truth).

Characteristics of Bad Design

1
Cluttered interface: Overcrowded screen with too many elements.
2
Poor navigation: Hard to find menus or move between pages.
3
Confusing layout: Disorganized placement of buttons and content.
4
Complex tasks: Simple goals require too many difficult steps.
5
Irrelevant user feedback: Unhelpful or confusing system alerts.
6
Inconsistent experience: Controls change look or behavior across screens.
7
Difficult content: Hard-to-read text or confusing jargon.
8
Unpleasant color scheme: Harsh, clashing colors that hurt readability.
VS

Characteristics of Good Design

1
Transparent and visible to users: Actions and system status are clearly seen.
2
Simple: Clean, minimal, and focused only on what matters.
3
Relevant: Information directly matches the user’s current need.
4
Usable: Easy and effortless for anyone to operate.
5
Problem solver: Directly resolves the user’s actual task or issue.
6
Easy to understand: Self-explanatory without needing instructions.
7
Effective to meet the goals: Reliably achieves the desired outcome.
8
Fresh with pleasant color scheme: Visually balanced and comfortable to view.
02
Definition & Goal

What is Interaction Design (IxD) & Its Goal

1
Interaction Design (IxD): Designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives. It is the central axis of UX work for digital products, and also applies to non-digital products.
2
Dialogue Definition: The creation of a physical and emotional dialogue between a person and a product, system, or service. This dialogue unfolds over time through the interplay of form, function, and technology.
3
Main Goal of IxD (Useful, Usable, and Satisfying): Systems must solve real needs (useful), be easy to operate (usable), and feel rewarding to use (satisfying). All three qualities must be present for an interactive product to succeed with users.
03
1D to 5D Framework

Five Dimensions of Interactive Design

1D — Words: Text elements such as button labels that give users the right amount of information. Words should be simple and clear so users immediately understand what a control does.
2D — Visual representations: Graphical elements like images, typography, and icons that aid interaction. Visuals work alongside text so users can recognize options quickly at a glance.
3D — Physical objects / space: The hardware medium and physical space through which users interact with the product. Examples include using a laptop via a physical mouse or a mobile phone via fingers on a screen.
4D — Time: Media that changes over time, such as animations, videos, and sounds. Includes motion transitions, audio alerts, and how users track progress over time.
5D — Behavior: How the first four dimensions define the actions a user performs and how the product reacts with feedback. Covers both how users operate the system and how the system responds to their inputs.
04
Core Components

Core Components of Interaction Design

1. Users
1
Who uses the system: The target people interacting with the product. Designers study their skills, needs, and limitations.
2. Tasks
2
What they want to do: The specific goals and activities to complete. Workflows are structured around these steps.
3. Context of Use
3
Where interaction happens: The physical, social, and device setting. Shapes how the interface works in real-world conditions.
05
Design Principles

Key Principles of Interaction Design

1
Visibility: Users should easily see what actions are possible. Important buttons and options are placed in plain sight instead of hidden in secret menus.
2
Feedback: The system informs users about what is happening after an action. Visual highlights, loading bars, or confirmation messages show that a click worked.
3
Consistency: Similar actions behave in the same way throughout the system. Keeping icons, colors, and menus uniform makes the interface predictable and easy to learn.
4
Constraints: Limits that prevent users from making mistakes. Examples include graying out a Submit button until all required fields are filled.
5
Affordances: Design elements suggest how they are used. A raised 3D button visually suggests pushing/clicking, while a slider suggests dragging.
06
Design Process

The Interaction Design Process

1
Identify User Needs: Understand the users, their needs, and the problems to be solved. Gathered through User interviews, Surveys, Observation of users, Task analysis, and Stakeholder meetings.
2
Develop Alternative Designs: Create multiple possible solutions to address user needs. Includes exploring Different layouts, Various navigation structures, and Alternative interaction methods.
3
Build Interactive Prototypes: Create an early version of the system to test ideas before full development. Helps designers Visualize how the system will work, Identify usability problems early, and Gather feedback from users and stakeholders.
4
Design Evaluation: Test the design using Usability Testing, Expert Evaluation, and User Feedback. A. Usability Testing: real users perform tasks on the prototype • B. Expert Evaluation: usability experts analyze the design • C. User Feedback: collect comments and suggestions from users.
5
Iteration and Refinement: Revise and improve the system based on evaluation results. Interaction design is not a one-time process; teams repeat testing and fixing until the product works well.

Lesson 4: User-Centered Design (UCD)

01
UCD Overview

What is User-Centered Design (UCD)?

1
User-Centered Design (UCD): An iterative design process where designers focus on users and their needs in each phase of development. It involves users throughout the entire product lifecycle, from initial planning to post-release assessment.
2
Key to Being Truly User-Centric: Continuously collect feedback and iterate on designs accordingly. Ongoing iteration future-proofs the product and keeps it relevant to users over time.

Human-Centered Design (HCD)

1
Broad & Holistic: Focuses on general human needs, emotions, and perspectives. Looks at the whole person and broader societal impact across every step.
VS

User-Centered Design (UCD)

2
Specific & Task-Focused: Focuses on a specific target audience and their direct tasks. Requires deep knowledge of the exact target users who will operate the product.
02
Purpose & Benefits

Why User-Centered Design?

Main Goal of UCD: Create products that users find useful (relevance) and usable (ease-of-use), cutting costs while increasing satisfaction and productivity.
Questions UCD Seeks to Answer
1
What is important to users?
2
What tasks users do, how frequently, and in what order?
3
What is the users’ work environment?
4
What are the users’ problems and constraints?
5
What are users’ expectations in terms of functionality?
6
What output is required and in what form?
7
How can the design facilitate users’ cognitive processes?
Business Benefits of UCD
1
Easy-to-use products: Builds intuitive products that require minimal effort to operate.
2
Satisfy customers: Meets real user goals, increasing loyalty and happiness.
3
Decrease expenditures on technical support and training: Fewer user mistakes mean lower help-desk and training costs.
4
Advertise ease-to-use successes: Great usability becomes a strong selling point in marketing.
5
Ultimately increase market share: Better products attract more buyers and outperform competitors.
03
Principles & Elements

UCD Principles & Essential Elements

Core Quote: “People ignore design that ignores people.”
Important UCD Principles
1
Involve users from the very beginning: Evaluate critical design decisions based on how they work for end-users right from the start. Never wait until the end of development to test if users understand the product.
2
User-centered design should be empathetic: Step into your user’s shoes and focus on solving their pain points. Prioritize fixing real user struggles rather than just launching what is easy to build.
3
Use an iterative design process: Go through repeated cycles (Define → Ideate / Prototype / User Test → Build & Deploy → Analyse & Iterate). New discoveries about users may require looping back to change fundamental parts of the design.
4
Involve multiple feedback loops: Gather qualitative and quantitative data at multiple steps (Action → Effect → Feedback). Collect frequent feedback from both internal team members and external end-users.
5
Follow the fundamentals of design: Keep things simple and think like your user. Apply core visual design rules like clean layout, contrast, and hierarchy so the interface stays clear.
Essential Elements of UCD
1
Visibility: Users see from the beginning what they can do with the product, what it is about, and how to use it. The purpose and main actions of the interface are immediately obvious on screen.
2
Accessibility: Users can find information easily and quickly. Provides multiple ways to locate content, such as Call-to-Action (CTA) buttons, search bars, and menus.
3
Legibility: Text must be easy to read. Uses clean fonts, readable sizes, and clear contrast so users never strain their eyes.
4
Language: Short sentences and easy words are preferred. Simple, everyday phrasing lets users understand instructions instantly.
04
Research Methods

User-Centered Design Methods

1
Focus Groups: A group of intended users collectively share thoughts and opinions to define product use cases. Characteristics: Multiple perspectives at once • Qualitative data • Small sample size • Low cost • Needs an experienced moderator.
2
Questionnaires and Surveys: Written questions used to obtain a large amount of statistical data on user challenges. Characteristics: Brief/simple feedback • Qualitative & Quantitative data • Larger sample size • Low cost • Questions must be unbiased.
3
Interviews: One-on-one open conversations in the early stages to dig for in-depth individual insights. Characteristics: Deep individual needs/behaviors • Mostly Qualitative • Small sample size • Time-consuming (high opportunity cost).
4
Usability Testing: Users interact with the product directly while a moderator records feedback. Characteristics: Requires a developed prototype • Qualitative & Quantitative • Small-to-medium sample • High cost live, cheaper with tools.
5
Card Sorting: Users sort content cards into groups to test and design the overall architecture of a website or app. Characteristics: Matches how users think • Quantitative data • Medium sample size • Time-consuming • Steps: Get stack → Sort into groups → Label groups.
6
Participatory Design: Actively involves users as co-designers alongside the product team during design workshops. Users help sketch and shape solutions directly so the final interface matches their real workflow.
05
UCD Process

The UCD Process: Research, Ideation & Validation

Stage 1: Research (“Who We Design For & How They Use It”)
1
Personas (“Who uses our product?”): Archetypes of real users representing a group with similar behaviors, needs, goals, and skills. Personas bring users to life so designers make right decisions on features, navigation, and visual design.
2
Interactions / Context of Use (“How will they use it?”): Always start with a problem a user faces, since people use products as solutions to problems. Highlight 3 scenario factors: User environment (where it happens), Medium (what device/screen size), and Mood (emotional state).
Stage 2: Concept Ideation (Visualizing Holistic Interactions)
1
User Journey Map: A timeline that visualizes how a user interacts with a product from the user’s point of view. Shows each phase (e.g., Consider, Explore, Compare, Test, Negotiate), user actions, and emotional highs and lows.
2
Storyboarding: Sequential comic-strip illustrations showing a user interacting with the product in real life. Helps teams see how the product fits into the user’s physical environment and existing routine.
Stage 3: Validation (Constant Activity Along the Way)
1
Usability Testing: Evaluates design decisions with actual users to see what problems they face when interacting with the design. Direct observation reveals interface flaws before and during development.
2
Contextual Inquiry: Observing and interviewing people while they perform tasks in context (in their natural environment). A mix of qualitative research and interviews where participants talk aloud about what they are doing as they work.
Key Takeaways on UCD
1
Turning empathy concepts into specific product requirements.
2
No guessing, no personal opinions—rely on real user data.
3
Involving all team members in product design.
4
Connecting UCD and business success.
